1 · Overview

What you are building

A weather-protected box that holds a Raspberry Pi with a camera and two microphones. A small radiation shield beside it carries the temperature, humidity and pressure sensor, and a rain plate sits on the roof. The Pi does no wildlife recognition itself. It offers the camera and microphone as one live stream, and the sensor readings, on the local network; the FALCON computer collects them, does the analysis, stores the records and controls the node.

Small parts and consumables

  • Brass heat-set inserts: 10 × M3 (about 5.7 mm long) and 4 × M2.5. These press into the plastic with a hot soldering iron and give strong threads.
  • Screws: 6 × M3 × 12 (cover), 4 × M3 × 8 (tray), 4 × M2.5 × 6 (Pi), 4 × M2 × 6 self-tapping (camera), 2 × M3 × 20, 4 × M3 × 12 and 1 × M3 × 10 with nuts (hood, cradle, shield), 3 × M3 threaded rod 70 mm with 6 nuts (shield stack), 4 × M4 × 12 with nuts (pole plate).
  • Cable glands: 2 × M20 and 2 × M12 (PG7).
  • A clear acrylic or glass disc, 28 mm across and 2 mm thick, for the camera window. Clear silicone to seal it.
  • About 70 cm of 3 × 3 mm closed-cell foam strip, for the cover seal.
  • Fine insect mesh and thin acoustic fabric (speaker-grille cloth works), for the vents and microphone grilles.
  • A 4-core cable about 50 cm long for the BME280, and two hose clamps for a 40–90 mm pole.
  • Heat-shrink tubing and a few small cable ties.

Tools

Soldering iron (with a heat-set insert tip if you have one), solder, wire strippers, small screwdrivers, a multimeter, a 7 mm nut driver or pliers, and a computer with an SD card reader.

4 · Layout

What goes where inside the box

Everything electronic mounts on the printed tray while it is on your bench. The Raspberry Pi sits with its USB and Ethernet ports facing down toward the cable glands, and its components facing the front cover. Mounted that way, its 40-pin header runs down the right-hand side, so the audio HAT sits on the right and its microphones face the cover grilles.

5 · Power

Power: two ways to feed the node

Mains safety: the USB-C supply and the PoE injector are indoor devices. Keep them, and every mains connection, inside a dry building. Only low-voltage cables go outdoors. Use a finished, earthed C13 cord for the injector; a rewireable plug is not a substitute.
6 · Wiring

Wiring the sensors

Only seven wires are needed. The audio HAT already uses the I²S audio pins, a button pin, two LED pins and the HAT ID pins, so the sensors use pins it leaves free or can share. The BME280 shares the I²C bus with the audio chip: they have different addresses (the audio chip is 0x1A, the BME280 is 0x76 or 0x77), so both work at once. The rain controller's digital output goes to GPIO17.

Reaching the pins with the HAT fitted

The HAT's socket faces down onto the Pi, so it covers every pin and nothing sticks out on top for a jumper. Each socket leg does come through the HAT, though, as a small solder joint on its top face, in exactly the same layout as the pins below. Solder the seven wires to those joints.

  1. Find pin 1 on the HAT.Hold the HAT the way it sits on the Pi in the box (header on the right, as in the layout figure). Pin 1 is the top joint of the left column. Check with the multimeter: pin 1 to pin 6 reads 3.3 V when the Pi is powered.
  2. Cut and tin seven 15 cm wires.Cut female jumper leads in the suggested colours, keeping the female end. Strip 2 mm at the cut end and tin it.
  3. Solder each wire to its joint.Touch the tinned wire to the joint with a hot, clean iron for one or two seconds. Do not bridge to the neighbouring joint; check with the multimeter's continuity beeper.
  4. Label and secure the wires.Label both ends (a strip of tape and a pen is enough), then run the bundle along the HAT's right edge and fix it to the tray with a tie so a pull cannot rip a joint.

BME280

  • Power it from 3.3 V only (pin 1). This 3.3 V board has no voltage regulator, so 5 V would damage it.
  • Connect four wires: VCC (or VIN), GND, SCL, SDA. Leave CSB and SDO unconnected at first.
  • After power-on, i2cdetect -y 1 should show 76 or 77. If it shows neither, tie CSB to 3.3 V (this selects I²C) and SDO to GND (this fixes the address at 0x76).
  • Use the 4-core cable between the shield and the box, with the jumper leads' female ends on the sensor pins. Seal each joint with heat-shrink.

Rain plate and controller

  • The plate has two pins; connect them to the controller's two plate pins with the supplied leads (either way round). The plate sits on the roof, while the controller stays dry inside the box.
  • Power the controller from 3.3 V (pin 17), never 5 V: at 3.3 V its DO output can never exceed what the Pi's GPIO accepts. The seller's listing contradicts itself (3.3–5 V in the text, 5 V in the table), so check with the multimeter that DO reads close to 3.3 V dry and close to 0 V wet before connecting it to pin 11.
  • Most of these boards pull DO low when wet, and their small LED lights up. If yours is the other way round, set FALCON_RAIN_WET_WHEN_LOW=false on the Pi.
  • Turn the blue sensitivity screw so the LED stays off when dry and switches on with a few drops of water.

Camera ribbon and audio HAT

  • The camera ribbon goes into the Pi socket marked CAMERA (between the micro-HDMI ports and the audio jack). Lift the socket's latch, insert the ribbon with its metal contacts facing the micro-HDMI ports, and press the latch down. The ribbon comes already connected to the camera; leave that end as it is.
  • The HAT stacks on the Pi with the supplied M2.5 × 15 standoffs. If the supplied 2 × 20 header is an extension (socket on the bottom, pins on top), it goes between the Pi and the HAT to bridge the gap. Leave the two speakers unconnected.
  • Fit the heatsinks before the HAT.
7 · Assembly

Assembly, step by step

The order matters in two places. The roof-hood and shield-arm bolts have nuts inside the box, so fit them before the tray goes in. And test everything on the bench before closing anything.

  1. Check the prints and set the inserts.Press the M3 inserts into the six cover holes in the shell rim and the four tray bosses on its back wall; press the M2.5 inserts into the four Pi bosses on the tray. Use the iron at about 220 °C and push them in straight until they sit flush.
  2. Test the electronics loose on the bench.Set up the Pi (section 8) with the camera, HAT and sensors connected by loose leads, and go through the checks in section 10. Fix problems now, while everything is reachable.
  3. Build the tray.Fit the heatsinks, screw the Pi to its four bosses (ports toward the tray's bottom edge), add the four 15 mm standoffs and the HAT, and solder the seven sensor wires. Screw the camera to the tower with four M2 screws, lens facing out, and connect the ribbon. Tie the PoE splitter (if used) into its bay and the rain controller into its frame.
  4. Prepare the shell.Glue insect mesh inside the bottom and top vent slots. Fit the four cable glands in the bottom wall. Bolt the shield arm to the right-hand side wall and the roof hood to the top (nuts inside, both now, while the box is empty).
  5. Prepare the cover.Bed the clear disc into the recess behind the camera window with a thin ring of clear silicone, and glue acoustic fabric over the two microphone grilles on the inside. Stick the foam strip around the shell rim, just inside the screw holes.
  6. Feed the cables in.Push each cable through its gland before connecting it: the Ethernet or PoE cable through the left M20 gland, the USB-C cable through the second M20 (option A only), the BME280 cable through the third gland and the rain-plate wires through the fourth. Leave a drip loop below each gland so water drips off instead of running in.
  7. Install the tray.Connect the cables, then screw the tray to the shell's back-wall inserts with four M3 × 8 screws. Tighten the gland nuts until the seals grip the cables.
  8. Close the cover.Check that no wire lies across the rim, then fit the cover with six M3 × 12 screws. Tighten them evenly in a cross pattern so the foam compresses all round.
  9. Fit the rain plate and the shield.Bolt the cradle to the hood and lay the plate in its pocket, pins at the high end. Build the shield stack on the three threaded rods, with the BME280 tied to the carrier strip in the middle, then bolt the cap's tab under the arm.
  10. Mount the node.Bolt the pole-mount plate to the back with four M4 bolts and clamp it to the pole with two hose clamps, camera facing the area to watch. For a wall, skip the plate and screw the shell directly through its four back holes with rubber washers.
8 · Software on the node

Setting up the Raspberry Pi

The node software is in the FALCON source, in its node folder. It installs itself: you prepare the SD card on your computer, put it in the Pi and switch on. Internet is needed once, for the installation.

  1. Write the SD card.With Raspberry Pi Imager choose Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit). In its settings set the hostname to falcon-node, choose a user and password, add your Wi-Fi network if you will not use Ethernet, and turn on SSH.
  2. Put the FALCON node software on the card.Leave the card in your computer. A drive called bootfs appears. In the FALCON folder run the line below (on Windows use the card's drive letter, for example E:). It copies the software, creates the node's settings and prints a pairing key. Write it down; it is also saved on the card in falcon-node/PAIRING.txt. 
    python3 node/sdcard/prepare_sdcard.py \
    /media/$USER/bootfs --name "Backyard node"
  3. Start the node.Fit the audio HAT and connect the camera and sensors before switching on (the HAT is only recognized at power-on). Put the card in the Pi and switch on with internet available. The node installs its software (about 10 minutes), restarts once and starts streaming. The installation log is written to falcon-node/install.log on the card.
  4. Without the card tool.On a Pi that is already running, copy the node folder onto it and run one command. It prints the address and the pairing key when it finishes; run it again at any time to update. 
    sudo bash node/install.sh
  5. Check what the node found.Open http://falcon-node.local:8780 in a browser on the same network: the status page lists the camera, microphone, weather sensor and rain plate. On the Pi itself: 
    systemctl status falcon-node          # running?
rpicam-hello --list-cameras           # imx708 listed
arecord -l                            # audio HAT listed as card Zero
i2cdetect -y 1                        # 1a (audio codec) and 76 or 77 (BME280)
pinctrl get 17                        # rain: hi when dry, lo when wet
9 · Connecting to FALCON

Connecting the node to FALCON

  1. Pair the node.On the FALCON computer open Sensors → Add sensor node. Enter falcon-node.local (or the Pi's IP address) and the pairing key. FALCON shows the node with its camera, microphone and sensors and starts collecting weather readings.
  2. Watch it.Open Live, choose the node and press Start observing. Detections appear on the right; confirm or correct them with one click.
  3. Fix the focus.The node watches one fixed scene, so autofocus would only hunt on moving leaves. In Sensors → Camera picture settings set Focus to "Fixed distance" and move the slider while watching Live (0.5 is about 2 m, 0 is infinity). Brightness, white balance, HDR and flip are set there too.
  4. Start by itself.In Sensors → Start automatically switch it on and choose the node, so FALCON resumes observing after a power cut. The node itself always starts streaming on its own.
  5. Add domestic animals to the species list.Cattle, goats, camels and dogs will appear on camera, but research-grade wildlife lists leave them out, so FALCON would label a cow as the nearest wild species. Add them once: make taxa ARGS=--include-domestic.

FALCON always connects to the node, never the other way round. So it works when FALCON runs under WSL on Windows or behind a firewall, and the node needs no keys or addresses of the FALCON computer. The pairing key never travels over the network: FALCON signs its requests with it.

11 · Limits

What this PoC hardware cannot do yet

These are known gaps of the current parts, to be fixed when the hardware is upgraded. None of them stops the proof of concept.

  • No night vision. Camera Module 3 Standard blocks infrared and there is no IR lamp, so nights are dark, when much mammal activity happens. The NoIR version and an 850 nm illuminator fix this later.
  • Muffled microphones. The HAT's microphones sit behind the cover grille; an external microphone with a windscreen will hear far better.
  • Humidity in dew. The BME280 reads poorly once it is wet with dew; a sensor with a built-in heater (such as the SHT45) is a cheap upgrade.
  • Rain is only wet or dry. The plate cannot measure rainfall, stays wet with dew, and corrodes over time. A tipping-bucket gauge measures millimetres.
  • The SD card can corrupt on a power cut. A read-only root filesystem, or booting from a USB SSD, prevents this.
  • No clock of its own. The Pi 4 has no battery clock. Without internet it takes the time from FALCON as soon as FALCON connects, and corrects the readings it took before that.
